[[Daniel 7:1-4]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- As in Daniel 2 Babylon is the first kingdom in the progression, so also here. Instead of a head of gold, Babylon is now depicted as a lion (fittingly symbolizing Babylon’s strength and domination).
- Images of lions were discovered on Babylon’s processional walls and on the Ishtar Gate. The manes of these lions extend like eagle’s wings (the eagle has a formidable range and speed—something Babylon regularly showed in battle).

[[Daniel 7:5]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- The nation succeeding Babylon was the Medo-Persian Empire. It reigned from approximately 539 B.C. to 331 B.C. A powerful confederation, the Medo-Persian Empire is fittingly described here as a bear rising to strike its prey. The three ribs in its mouth represent the nations of Lydia, Babylon, and Egypt, three nations the Medes and Persians overran to obtain domination.

[[Daniel 7:6]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- The next world empire would be Greece, which conquered Medo-Persia in 331 B.C. and reigned until about 168 B.C. Under the leadership of Alexander the Great, Greece was noted for its aggression and phenomenal speed in moving its forces to the scene of battle (thus the symbolism of a leopard with not just two wings but four, symbolizing extraordinary swiftness).
- The four heads are intriguing, since in prophecy, heads—and sometimes also horns—usually indicate various divisions of leadership within or coming from a particular kingdom.

[[Daniel 7:7-8]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- History is abundantly clear that the Roman Empire conquered Greece in 168 B.C. and reigned until A.D. 476. Like the legs of iron in chapter 2, Daniel 7 depicts Rome as a beast having “huge iron teeth” and “devouring, breaking . . . , and trampling.” This description is certainly accurate, as few nations have ever matched the iron grip of ancient Rome.
- But strangely, Daniel spends relatively little time with the fourth beast itself. Instead, his attention turns to its horns, especially a “little one” that has human characteristics and speaks boastfully.

[[Daniel 7:9-10]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- Interrupting the symbolic description of nations throughout history comes this startling scene of the judgment of all humanity. The “Ancient of Days” is none other than the most high God, sometimes referred to simply as “our Father” (Isaiah 63:16; 64:8).
- Since He is seen on His throne the scene is in heaven, not on earth. A heavenly court, which will determine the fate of every person who has ever lived, is “seated,” showing its work is beginning. That’s why we see “the books” “opened.”
- The record of all that has transpired in human history from the dawn of time until the end is thus depicted. The judgment process requires such breadth of information because God seeks to bring as many people as possible into His kingdom and is looking for every available means to do so. No piece of evidence from any time or place in human history, therefore, will be excluded. Exactly when this judgment takes place is revealed later in the books of Daniel and Revelation.

[[Daniel 7:11-14]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- In chapter 3 we saw the “Son of Man” standing with the three faithful friends in Nebuchadnezzar’s fiery furnace. This “Son of Man” is Jesus Christ. During Jesus’ time on earth in the first century A.D., He was regularly referred to as both “the Son of God” and “the Son of Man.” These are divine titles that also show a profound connection to humanity. Jesus is fully God and fully human. How He can be both is a profound mystery that we will never be fully able to explain. But the results of this mystery are not baffling at all. Jesus’ becoming human while remaining fully divine uniquely qualifies Him to be both our Savior and our Judge—indeed, these are the roles that He plays in the heavenly judgment scene of Daniel 7, as we learn from the New Testament. God the Father presides over the heavenly court. Jesus acts as our Judge (John 5:22). But He is also our Savior, our Advocate, the one who intercedes for us and forgives us our sins (1 John 1:9; 2:1).
- This explains why Jesus is shown “coming with the clouds of heaven”—not to this earth, but into the courts of heaven (also sometimes called “the heavenly sanctuary”). The result of this judgment process is astonishing. Ultimately, it ushers in Jesus’ second coming to earth and the establishment of His kingdom that “shall not be destroyed.”

[[Daniel 7:15-18]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- Verse 17 interprets the beasts as four “kings.” However, as we saw in chapter 2, Nebuchadnezzar, though an individual king, was nonetheless representative of the whole of the Babylonian kingdom. Similarly, here in Daniel 7 these four kings represent the same four kingdoms we saw in Daniel 2.

[[Daniel 7:19-28]]

- {"style": {"block": {"backgroundColor": "#F1E5DA", "padding": {"top": "sm", "start": "base", "end":"sm", "bottom": "sm"}}}}
- A progression is here repeated three times: The little horn reigns à Heavenly judgment occurs à God’s kingdom is established and given to His people (see verses 8-14, 21, 22, and 24-27). This shows that God’s kingdom will not be established on earth until the heavenly judgment process is completed, something we’ll learn more about in chapter 8 and in the book of Revelation.
- Daniel was profoundly interested in the little horn that comes up from the ashes of the Roman Empire—and no wonder! It was prophesied to oppress the followers of God as no other power had ever done.
- Identifying this little horn is crucial to understanding the timing of both the judgment in heaven and the establishment of God’s kingdom on earth.
- Who is this little-horn power? This chapter reveals nine characteristics:
	1. It would come up “among them,” that is, among the 10 horns/nations of Western Europe (verse 8).
	2. It would arise after A.D. 476, when Rome finally fell to the last of these nations (verse 8).
	3. It starts out “little,” not a vast empire like the Greeks or the Romans (verse 8).
	4. Three horns or nations would be uprooted before it becomes a substantial power (verse 8).
	5. It would “speak against the Most High,” that is, it would speak pompous words/blasphemies against God (verses 8 and 25; see also Daniel 11:36).
	6. It would be “different” in substantial ways from other kingdoms (verse 24).
	7. It would “intend to change times and law” (verse 25). Human laws change all the time, so it cannot refer to them. But God’s times and laws are eternal. To “intend to change” God’s fixed times and laws would fulfill this prediction and explain its blasphemous behavior.
	8. It is a power that would “war against”/“persecute” God’s followers (verses 21 and 25).
	9. It would initially rule for “a time and times and half a time,” that is, three and a half years, or 1,260 days, with each day symbolizing one year. To discover why a day in prophecy often symbolizes a literal year, please **[click here 🔗](https://thingsnew.org/en/d07b/)**.
- There is only one power in history that fits all nine of these characteristics. It is extremely important to know who or what this power represents, as it plays a key role in history from God’s perspective.
- This power will be more specifically identified when we study Revelation 13, a passage that also deals with this power but in greater detail.
- Daniel 7 finishes with Daniel feeling “greatly troubled” (verse 28). The events he saw predicted in this vision, though shown to some extent in chapter 2, were astonishing in their weight and scope. The oppression God’s people would face through the centuries, and the enormity of the task God the Father and the Son would face as they sought to save as much of humanity as possible, seemed overwhelming to Daniel. He still firmly believed that God’s justice, goodness, and love would triumph! But the cost would be great, and Daniel’s heart sank as he thought about how long it would take.
