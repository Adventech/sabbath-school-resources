> Why I love the Sabbath

There’s something different about the Sabbath, and it is hard to explain unless you’ve experienced it the way I have. It doesn’t start loudly. There’s no sudden pause during which the world stops moving. People are still talking, the world keeps moving forward, and life still happens. But for me, everything changes the moment the Sabbath starts.

All week, life feels constant. There’s always something to do or something to worry about. School, expectations, responsibilities—they don’t really leave space for rest. Even when I’m not doing anything, my mind is still racing. I’m thinking about what I didn’t finish, what I could’ve done better, or what’s coming next.

But that all changes when the Sabbath comes.

I’ve grown up understanding that the Sabbath isn’t just a day off; it is something God created from the beginning—a day set apart and made holy. And to me, that idea changes everything. It means the Sabbath isn’t something I earn after a long week of work; it is something given to me, no matter what my week looked like.

That’s one of the reasons I love it so much. Because most of the time, rest feels like a reward. It feels like I deserve it only if I’ve worked hard enough or done everything right. But the Sabbath doesn’t work like that. It comes whether I feel ready for it or not, even if I feel behind, stressed, or overwhelmed. And when it does, it invites me to stop, not because everything is done, but because it doesn’t have to be.

At first, slowing down can be uncomfortable. I’m so used to always thinking, always checking something, always moving forward. The quiet can feel unfamiliar, almost as if I don’t know what to do with it. But over time I’ve started to realize that the quiet is where the Sabbath really begins to mean something.

It creates space. Space to breathe without rushing, to think without pressure. Space to reconnect, not just with the people around me, but with God. That’s something I don’t make enough time for during the week. But the Sabbath brings me back. It shows me what actually matters.

Going to church, listening to the sermon, singing, and spending time with the people around me; it all feels different on the Sabbath. Not forced, not like something I have to do, but like something meaningful. And even outside of church, the whole day feels set apart. Even small things feel as though they actually have space to exist.

I love the Sabbath because it reminds me who I am beyond everything else. During the week it is easy to define myself by my grades, my expectations, or how well I keep up. But the Sabbath reminds me that my value doesn’t come from any of that. It comes from the fact that I am a child of God, and that is enough.

It is comforting to know that no matter what happens, the Sabbath always comes again. No matter how stressful the week was, no matter how I feel about myself, I know there will be another Sabbath, another chance to pause, reset, and start again.

Life doesn’t always feel steady. Things change, people change, and even I change. But the Sabbath doesn’t. It stays the same, a reminder that God’s love and care stay the same no matter what I’m going through.

When the Sabbath ends, everything comes back: the responsibilities, the stress, the constant movement. But it doesn’t feel the same as it did before. It is as though I carry something with me _into_ the new week. Not something loud or obvious, but something quieter. A kind of stillness. A reminder that I don’t always have to keep running just because everything else is.

I don’t always experience the Sabbath perfectly. My mind still drifts at times as I think about all that is waiting for me when the new week begins. But even in those moments, when I notice my thoughts pulling me away, choosing to return to the Sabbath changes something. It creates space where there wasn’t any before. And so I continue to stay grounded in the peace that the Sabbath offers.

And maybe that’s what matters. Because for a little while the noise fades, and the pressure is gone. The world keeps moving, but somewhere between one week and the next, between one sunset and another, there is always a space waiting. A space in which I am not defined by what I’ve done, but simply by the fact that I am here, and that is enough.
