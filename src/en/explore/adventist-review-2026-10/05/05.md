> <p></p>
> God’s self-giving love transforms the way we worship and witness.

Every morning before dawn, a missionary quietly unlocked the gate of a small Christian school in the Middle East. Before classes could begin, another responsibility awaited him. During the night, villagers piled their household rubbish against the wall surrounding the school and entrance. Rotting food, broken glass, plastic, and other refuse formed a heap that often stood more than a meter high.

This was not an act of carelessness. In that cultural context, throwing garbage at someone’s property was a deliberate expression of rejection and contempt. The Christian school, along with the missionaries who served there, had become objects of disdain. Every pile of rubbish carried the same unspoken message: You are not welcome here.

Many would have found such treatment reason enough to leave. Others might have appealed to the authorities, demanded justice, or requested a different assignment. But the missionary chose a different response. Each morning he quietly picked up a shovel and began cleaning. He never confronted the villagers or protested. What many would have considered a humiliating task, he came to view as an act of worship.

As he removed the rubbish from the school entrance, he would sing and pray for the villagers, drawing inspiration from Jesus’ words: “The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve” (Matt. 20:28). Though the work was unpleasant, exhausting, and apparently unnoticed, he performed it dutifully. In those early mornings of humble service, he felt closer to Christ than ever before. True service is most Christlike when it is offered without recognition and without expecting anything in return.

For months this was his daily routine. Constant exposure to decaying waste, foul odors, and disease gradually weakened his health. Anyone could have questioned why he continued. Surely there were limits to what a person should endure. Yet he understood that before the villagers would trust the message he preached, they first needed to encounter the character of the Savior he served in the way he lived.

Gradually the piles of rubbish that once littered the school entrance vanished. People began greeting him warmly, and their initial suspicion transformed into genuine kindness. Over time families who had once rejected the Christian school entrusted their children to its teachers. No argument or public debate had persuaded them. They had witnessed something far more compelling than eloquent preaching—the quiet persistence of Christlike love.

This story captures the heart of the gospel. The missionary was doing far more than cleaning rubbish from the entrance of a school. His service became an offering of worship to the Lord, flowing naturally from his communion with Christ. In his quiet willingness to enter a place of rejection, remove what others had thrown there in resentment, and keep serving those who despised him, he was offering a living picture of the story of redemption. From Genesis to Revelation, “Scripture traces a consistent pattern: humanity fractures its relationship with God through disobedience, and God responds by entering into that brokenness to accomplish what humans cannot restore themselves.”<sup>1</sup> The gospel begins, not with humanity seeking God, but with God seeking humanity.

### The Offering God Gave First

After Adam and Eve sinned, shame and guilt drove them into hiding. The intimacy they had once enjoyed with their Creator gave way to fear. They attempted to cover themselves with fig leaves and concealed themselves among the trees of the garden. Yet while humanity hid, God came looking. The question “Where are you?” (Gen. 3:9) was not asked because God had lost sight of His children. It was an invitation to relationship.<sup>2</sup> Before there was an altar, a sanctuary, or a sacrificial system, there was a God who crossed the distance that sin had created. Even the garments of skin with which He clothed Adam and Eve pointed forward to the day He Himself would provide the perfect sacrifice.<sup>3</sup>

This divine initiative reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ. “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). In those few words, John describes the greatest act of self-giving in history. The eternal Son of God entered a world disfigured by sin. He did not remain at a safe distance from human suffering, nor did He wait for humanity to become worthy of His presence. He stepped into our brokenness, bore our shame, and carried the consequences of our rebellion.

The Incarnation, however, was not the final expression of Christ’s self-giving love. It was the path that led to Calvary. If Bethlehem reveals the depths to which God was willing to descend, Golgotha reveals how much He was willing to give. When Christians hear the word “offering,” we often think first of money placed in an offering plate or a gift given for the support of the church and the advancement of mission. Scripture begins somewhere deeper. Before God ever asks humanity to bring an offering, He reveals Himself as the great Giver (1 Chron. 29:14).<sup>4</sup>

Paul writes that Christ “has given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma” (Eph. 5:2). Jesus is not only the one who receives our worship; He is Himself the supreme offering, and in His self-giving love His worthiness to receive our worship is most fully revealed. Every sacrifice in the Old Testament anticipated Him. Every lamb placed upon the altar, every priestly act within the sanctuary, and every drop of sacrificial blood pointed forward to the day when God Himself would provide the offering no human being could provide.<sup>5</sup> At the cross the sacrificial system reached its fulfillment in the One who gave Himself for the life of the world.<sup>6</sup>

John expresses this truth in one of Scripture’s best-known declarations: “For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son” (John 3:16). God’s giving flows from His love. It was not a response to human worthiness, but the expression of who He is. The cross reveals that self-giving love is not merely something God does; it belongs to His very character. His grace precedes our obedience, and His sacrifice makes restored relationship possible.

This revelation reshapes our understanding of worship and giving. Christian giving is not a means of securing God’s favor, but the grateful response of those who have already received grace they could never earn. We give, not because God depends on what we possess, but because His love has reordered our hearts. Every genuine offering therefore bears witness to the supreme offering made in Christ.

### When Giving Becomes Worship

Jesus once observed worshipers as they brought their gifts to the temple treasury (Mark 12:41-44). Some gave large amounts. Then a poor widow approached and placed two small copper coins into the treasury. Financially, her gift seemed insignificant. Spiritually, it became unforgettable. Jesus declared that she had given more than all the others because heaven measures generosity differently from earth. People see gestures and amounts; God sees the motives of the heart.

The same truth appears in the story of Mary of Bethany. When she broke her alabaster flask and poured its costly perfume upon Jesus, some immediately calculated its value. To them, it appeared excessive, even wasteful. To Jesus, it was an act of devotion (Matt. 26:6-13). Mary was expressing a heart overwhelmed by gratitude. Having encountered the grace of Christ, she gave Him her best because love had become the measure of her worship.

The widow and Mary remind us that the value of an offering is never determined merely by its quantity. It is determined by the love from which it flows and the surrender it represents. This is why Christian giving must never be reduced to money alone. Tithes and offerings are sacred expressions of worship, but they belong within a larger offering of the whole life to God. Scripture calls us to present our bodies as “a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God” (Rom. 12:1). God desires not only what is in our hands, but the heart, the will, the time, the body, and the life from which every true offering comes.

Paul understood this when he wrote, “The love of Christ compels us” (2 Cor. 5:14). To encounter that love is to see everything differently. Possessions no longer provide our security, achievement no longer determines our worth, and service is no longer driven by the desire to be noticed. Christian giving, therefore, is not confined to what we place in an offering plate; it is the surrender of the whole life to Christ.

Here worship and mission converge. The Christ who crossed the distance between heaven and earth now calls His followers to cross the barriers that keep people from encountering His love. For some, this means crossing oceans; for others, simply crossing the street. Some proclaim the gospel to thousands, while others patiently guide a single searching person toward Christ. The expressions of the call may differ, but its heart remains the same: a life placed at God’s disposal for the sake of His mission.

### A Life Offered to God

Mission shaped by such self-giving love inevitably carries a cost. It demands our presence, patience, and, at times, a willingness to be misunderstood. It calls us to love without assurance of return, to serve without recognition, and to remain faithful when the fruit of our labor is not immediately visible. Such compassion is not sustained by human feeling alone; it grows out of communion with Christ. Those who have received the self-giving love of God are thereby enabled to extend that love to others.

Some of the greatest barriers to mission are not geographical, religious, or cultural, but lodged within the human heart. Pride, resentment, fear, prejudice, indifference, and self-protection can keep us at a distance from those whom Christ longs to restore.<sup>7</sup> Yet the gospel calls us beyond these boundaries. It sends us to the nations, but also toward the neighbor, the estranged relative, the wounded relationship, and the person we would rather avoid. Mission begins wherever the love of Christ leads us beyond the limits of our own comfort.

This is why Christian mission must be understood as worship. Worship is not confined to songs, prayers, sermons, or offerings presented during a service. These are sacred expressions of worship, but Scripture calls for something larger: a life presented to God. When we serve the broken, give sacrificially, care for our bodies, honor the Sabbath, use our influence for good, and enter another person’s pain with the compassion of Christ, we are not merely doing religious work. We are responding to the God who first gave Himself for us.

The missionary’s repeated act of clearing the school entrance revealed far more than a willingness to perform an unpleasant task. It embodied the heart of Christ. Before the villagers understood his theology, they encountered the character of his Savior. Before they listened to his message, they witnessed its meaning through his compassion.

Perhaps this is among the greatest gifts a believer can offer—not merely something placed in God’s hands, but a life so transformed by grace that others catch a glimpse of Christ through it. Such a life is always a response to the greater gift first given by God Himself: the One who entered our broken world, bore our shame, cleansed us from sin, and gave Himself to bring us home.
