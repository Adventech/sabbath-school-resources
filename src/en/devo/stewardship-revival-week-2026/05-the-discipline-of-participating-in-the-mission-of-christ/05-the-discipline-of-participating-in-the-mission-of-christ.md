### The Towel and the Basin

On the night before the cross, Jesus did something no one expected. As the disciples argued about who was greatest, Jesus rose from the table, wrapped a towel around His waist, and began washing their feet. The Creator washed the feet of His creation. The Master served His servants.

In that moment, Jesus revealed the heart of God. The world measures greatness by power, position, and recognition. Jesus measured greatness by service: “The greatest among you will be your servant” (Matthew 23:11, NIV).

Service was not merely something Jesus did. It was who He was. And if Christ came not to be served but to serve, then those who follow Him are called to do the same. After washing His disciples’ feet, Jesus explained, “For I have given you an example, that you should do as I have done to you” (John 13:15, NKJV).

This is one of the great truths of sanctified stewardship: God has entrusted us with time, abilities, spiritual gifts, and resources not merely for ourselves, but for the blessing of others. More than that, service is not merely the result of spiritual growth—it is one of God’s primary means of producing it. Ellen G. White beautifully expresses this truth: “[Christ] gives to us that we may minister to others and thus become like Himself.”<sup>1</sup>

We become more like Christ by serving as Christ served. For this reason, participating in Christ’s mission is not an optional activity for a few committed believers. It is a discipline for every disciple and one of God’s chosen pathways for sanctification.

#### ^[The Stewardship of Our Lives]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Stewardship is not simply about preserving what God has entrusted to us—it is about placing it at His disposal for the blessing of others and the advancement of His kingdom. As Paul reminds us, “We are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them” (Ephesians 2:10, NKJV).

When many people hear the word stewardship, they think immediately of money. Yet Scripture presents a much larger vision. Every believer has been entrusted with resources that extend far beyond finances: time, strength, talents, spiritual gifts, and influence. These are not possessions to be consumed, but trusts to be invested in God’s mission.

Some teach, organize, lead, or quietly serve behind the scenes. God distributes gifts according to His wisdom so every believer may contribute to His mission. Are you using what God has entrusted to you for His mission?

Service is where stewardship becomes visible. When we offer our time, strength, abilities, spiritual gifts, and influence for the good of others, stewardship moves from theory to practice. What God has placed in our hands begins to flow through our lives. And as it does, something remarkable happens. We not only bless others—we ourselves are changed.

#### ^[Created to Give, Not Merely Receive]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

The apostle Paul exhorts believers, “Through love serve one another” (Galatians 5:13, NKJV). Christian service is not driven by guilt or obligation, but by love flowing from a heart transformed by Christ.

Trees provide one of God’s most beautiful illustrations of the Christian life. A healthy tree receives nourishment and bears fruit for others. Likewise, Christians receive God’s blessings not merely for themselves, but so those blessings may flow outward in service. Healthy disciples naturally bear fruit because God’s blessings were never meant to stop with them. This is why Jesus frequently used the language of fruitfulness. A healthy tree naturally bears fruit, and a healthy disciple naturally serves.

#### ^[Service as a Means of Grace]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Jesus declared, “He who believes in Me, . . . out of his heart will flow rivers of living water” (John 7:38, NKJV). God’s grace was never intended to terminate with us but to flow through us into the lives of others.

Most Christians understand that prayer changes us, Scripture changes us, and worship changes us. But service changes us too.

Harry Emerson Fosdick once wrote, “Electricity may not come in where it cannot flow through.”<sup>2</sup> God’s grace is not given merely to be accumulated, but to be shared. Ellen G. White suggests, “In working to bless others we shall be gathering strength and courage to our own souls.”<sup>3</sup> God pours into those who pour out.

This truth is beautifully illustrated by the Sea of Galilee and the Dead Sea. Both receive water from the Jordan River, yet only one is full of life. The difference is simple: The Sea of Galilee gives what it receives, while the Dead Sea keeps it. The lesson is more than geography. It is a parable of the spiritual life. When grace flows outward through acts of service, faith becomes active, love deepens, and Christ becomes more real.

One of the quiet surprises of Christian service is joy. As we pour ourselves out for others, we discover God filling us at the same time. Grace stagnates when hoarded and grows when shared. As grace flows through us, the Holy Spirit forms the character of Christ. Service does not merely change the lives of those we serve. It changes the one who serves. Grace grows as it flows.

#### ^[The Samaritan Way]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

In Luke 10:29, a lawyer asked Jesus, “Who is my neighbor?” Beneath the question was an attempt to define the limits of responsibility. How far does love have to go? Who deserves my concern? Where does my obligation end?

Jesus answered with a story. A man was beaten and left beside the road. A priest and a Levite passed him by, but a Samaritan stopped, cared for him, and met his needs. The Samaritan did not ask whether the wounded man deserved help. He did not calculate the inconvenience. He simply responded to the need before him. This is the heart of Christlike service.

The question is not merely, “Who is my neighbor?” The better question is: “Who is God placing in front of me?” Service often arrives disguised as interruption.

The Samaritan teaches us two enduring truths: love acts and compassion moves.

Jesus ended the parable with a simple command: “Go and do likewise” (Luke 10:37). The lesson remains unchanged. Service is measured by a willingness to enter another person’s need with the love of Christ.

This truth becomes even clearer in Matthew 25:35, 36, where Jesus identifies Himself with those in need: “I was hungry and you gave Me food; I was thirsty and you gave Me a drink; I was a stranger and you took me in; . . . I was sick and you visited Me.” Then He declares, “Inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me” (Matthew 25:40, NKJV). Every act of kindness becomes an act of service to Christ Himself.

True spirituality is not measured only by what happens in worship, but by what happens when worship is over.

#### ^[Service Forms the Servant]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Paul urges believers, “Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus” (Philippians 2:5, NKJV). As we humble ourselves in service, the Holy Spirit forms within us the character of Christ.

Many believers assume they must become mature before serving. They wait until they feel prepared, gifted, or spiritually developed. Yet Scripture presents a different picture: Service itself is one of God’s chosen means of producing that growth.

The path to spiritual maturity is not found only in learning, praying, or worshiping. It is also found in serving. This is one of the great insights of sanctified stewardship: Service is not merely doing good. It is becoming good. As we participate in the mission of Christ, God is doing more than blessing those we serve. He is shaping the one who serves.

Service has a way of exposing what remains hidden within us. It humbles pride, disrupts selfishness, softens judgment, and develops humility, compassion, generosity, patience, and love through the transforming work of the Holy Spirit. Service does not merely change the world around us; it changes the person within us.

#### ^[Practicing the Discipline]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Participating in the mission of Christ does not require a title, a position, or a public platform. Service begins wherever we are and with whatever God has placed in our hands. Some key aspects of serving are:

1. **Notice.** Service begins by seeing. Many needs remain unmet because they remain unnoticed. Jesus consistently saw people others overlooked—the blind, the lonely, the poor, the grieving, and the forgotten.
2. **Enter In.** Seeing a need is not enough. The Samaritan did more than notice the wounded traveler; he entered his situation. Compassion moved him toward the need rather than away from it.
3. **Serve.** Do something. The size of the act matters far less than the willingness of the heart.

Service will cost something. It requires time, attention, empathy, and inconvenience. Yet it also brings unexpected joy. Time and again, those who serve discover that they often receive more than they give.

#### ^[A Serving Church]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Imagine a church where needs are noticed, gifts are used, compassion is normal, and service is expected. Such a church becomes a powerful witness because the character of Christ is visible among its people. A church that serves becomes a church that grows—not merely numerically, but spiritually.

Service is not just something Christians do. It is one of God’s primary ways of shaping who we become. We become Christlike by serving as Christ served.

1. In what ways does your current lifestyle support—or hinder—your ability to participate more fully in God’s work?
2. Which area of physical stewardship most needs attention right now: nutrition, exercise, rest, or temperance?
3. How might caring for your body become an act of worship and cooperation with God?

### ^[Appeal]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#5E0C1E"}}})

Perhaps you have been waiting until you feel ready to serve. But service is often the place where God prepares us.

The invitation today is simple: Pick up the towel and participate in the mission of Christ.

As Ellen G. White reminds us, “The strongest argument in favor of the gospel is a loving and lovable Christian.”<sup>4</sup>

Will you place your time, strength, gifts, and influence in God’s hands and allow Him to use you in His mission?
