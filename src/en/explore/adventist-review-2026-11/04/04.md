> A personal testimony of prayer, power, and the pursuit of grace

It was a normal Friday evening in Trinidad. The kind of evening during which the air hangs warm and still and nothing particularly remarkable seems likely to happen. I was walking with some friends when I noticed a large tent set up near the road. Music drifted out from inside, voices lifted together in song, and something I cannot fully explain compelled me to stop and step through the entrance.

I did not know then that this was the beginning of a journey that would change everything—my family, my faith, my entire understanding of who God is and how He works in the lives of ordinary people. I was a young Muslim man, raised in the traditions my father’s family had brought from India. I was not looking for conversion. I was merely curious.

That evening the minister made an announcement: anyone who brought five or more guests to the meetings would receive a free book. The offer intrigued me. The next night I returned with an armful of family members and friends. I collected my book. Then, having satisfied my curiosity, I stayed away.

But my family kept going.

Among those who continued attending was my older brother, George. Night after night he sat under that tent, listening, asking questions, weighing what he was hearing against everything he had ever been taught. And what he heard began to change him. The weeks passed, and the change grew undeniable.

One day George came to my father with an announcement of his own: he intended to be baptized as a Seventh-day Adventist.

My father’s response was swift and absolute. If George went ahead with this decision, he would no longer be welcome in our home. The threat was not idle. In our culture, in our faith community, in the family my father had spent his life building, what George was proposing was more than a personal choice. It was a rupture. A betrayal. A shame that could not easily be undone.

I added my own voice to my father’s. I pleaded with my brother. I begged him to reconsider, not because I believed he was wrong about everything he had heard, but because I knew what this decision would cost him—cost all of us.

George listened to everything I said. Then he was baptized anyway.

He was thrown out of our home the same week.

Then tragedy arrived.

### The Only Name That Worked

My 15-year-old sister lay motionless on her bed. It happened without warning, without explanation. She would not eat. She would not drink. She lay there as though life itself had stepped back from her body and was waiting on the threshold, uncertain whether to return.

We took her to doctors. We sought specialists. We emptied our finances pursuing every medical avenue we could find. Nothing helped. No diagnosis came. No treatment offered relief. The medical establishment looked at her and came away with only uncertainty.

So we turned to religion.

We invited three imams to our home. They came with incense and ritual prayers, with the weight of centuries of Islamic tradition behind them. They performed everything the tradition called for. When they left, my sister had not moved.

We went to Hindu temples. The priests prayed with sincere devotion. They invoked the names of gods who had been worshipped long before any of us were born. When we carried my sister home, she was the same.

Desperate, we drove her up the mountain to a Catholic monastery. A priest came out and sprinkled holy water over her, and, strangely, temporarily, she showed signs of improvement. We drove home filled with cautious hope. But before we reached the bottom of the mountain, my sister began speaking in a heavy, strange voice that made our blood run cold. Whatever was holding her was not going to release its grip easily.

Baptist ministers prayed over her. Pentecostal believers interceded with everything they had. Our home became a procession of earnest, well-meaning people, each carrying their tradition, each leaving in silence. Nothing changed.

Through all of this, George had made a quiet request. He asked if he could bring someone from his new church—an elder, he said—who wanted to pray for our sister.

We refused. We had not yet forgiven him for what he had done to our family. And besides, so many had already tried.

But eventually, desperation overruled pride. When there was nothing left to try, we agreed: Let George bring his elder.

I remember the day clearly.

A man arrived at our door. I will be honest with you: I was not a gracious host. Before he had even crossed the threshold, I confronted him.

“So many churches,” I said. “So many people. All of them have prayed for my sister. What difference can you make?”

He did not argue with me. He did not defend himself or his Adventist Church or his God. He simply looked at me and said, “Take me to her room.”

We walked up the stairs together. When we entered the room in which my sister lay, he asked if we could kneel in prayer. I had never knelt in prayer like that before, not in that position, not with that posture of complete humility before God. Something in me resisted, but I knelt.

He asked us to close our eyes.

I closed one eye and kept the other open.

The elder began to pray. His voice was calm, unhurried, utterly without theatrics. He did not shout or perform. He prayed the way that a man who is personally acquainted with the One he is speaking to would pray.

And then I closed both eyes.

I cannot tell you exactly why. Something in the atmosphere of that room shifted. Something in the quality of his prayer demanded that I be present for it fully, not as a skeptic watching from behind a partially opened eye, but as a person in a room in which God was being addressed.

Then he said words I will carry with me until I die: “Satan, I rebuke you in the name of Jesus.”

Immediately—not gradually, not eventually, but immediately—something happened.

My sister, who had not eaten or drunk anything for days, sat up. She looked around the room with clear eyes. And she asked for something to eat and drink.

We stood there in silence, unable to speak.

As I write these words, my sister has never suffered from that condition again.

### The Ripple Effect

What happened that afternoon in that small room changed our family forever. Not all at once—the journey of faith is rarely so neat—but the stone had been thrown into still water, and the ripples would continue long after the original splash. In the years that followed, hundreds of our Muslim and Hindu relatives came to faith in Jesus Christ. A family that had carried its religious traditions across an ocean from India, through generations, through every hardship and triumph, began, one by one, to bend the knee to a different name.

For me, however, understanding did not come immediately. I had witnessed something I could not explain and could not deny, and the combination of those two realities was almost more than I could hold.

Two weeks later I left Trinidad for Canada. I told myself I was leaving for opportunity, for a new life. But deep within me I knew I was also leaving because I needed distance to think, to wrestle with what I had seen.

The religion my father’s family had brought from India, the faith I had been raised in, the tradition that had shaped every aspect of our household, had been silent in our family’s greatest hour of need. And then one man spoke five words—“in the name of Jesus”—and my sister rose from her bed.

I could not argue with that. I could be confused by it. I could be unsettled by it. But I could not argue with it.

My own journey toward faith in Christ unfolded over time through honest searching, through study, through conversations I did not expect to find myself having.

I came to Christ at 21 years of age. And in the years since, as I have stood at bedsides and gravesides, prayed with the desperate and the doubting, walked with people through the deepest valleys of their lives, I have come back again and again to what I know—not merely as doctrine, but as lived experience: There is power in the name of Jesus. Not a theoretical or metaphorical power, but a real, immediate, present-tense power that shows up in the places in which everything else has already failed.

I often think about George, my brother who was willing to lose his home rather than silence what God had done in him. I think about what it cost him to make that choice, and what his courage, in the end, made possible. Sometimes the person who suffers most for their faith becomes the doorway through which others find it. George was that doorway for our family.

I think about that elder, too, who walked into a home in which he was not welcome, and knelt on the floor of a sick girl’s bedroom with no drama and no performance. He did not know what would happen. He simply prayed. He placed a name into the room and trusted the One who owned that name to show up.

When every avenue of human effort has been exhausted, when the room is quiet and hope is thin and you are kneeling on the floor of your last resort, the name of Jesus still works.

It worked for my sister. It worked for my family. It worked for me.

It will work for you.
