### A Gift Before the Fall

Before there was sin, there was Sabbath. Before it was a commandment carved in stone, the Sabbath was a gift woven into Creation—an invitation to live in rhythm with the Creator.

Humanity’s first full day was not a day of labor, but a day of rest in fellowship with God. Before they worked, they worshiped. Before they produced, they received. Before they labored, they were loved. “Then God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it” (Genesis 2:3, NKJV, emphasis added).

Week after week, God invites us to step out of a world driven by performance and productivity and enter a sacred space where our identity is no longer defined by what we accomplish, but by whose we are.

Sabbath remains one of God’s greatest gifts for spiritual formation. It teaches us to trust rather than strive, to receive rather than perform, and to rest in our identity as God’s children rather than prove our worth.

#### ^[Stewarding Time by Stopping]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Of all the gifts God has entrusted to us, time may be the easiest to waste and the hardest to steward. Unlike money or possessions, time cannot be saved or stored. Once spent, it is gone forever. Every day arrives as a gift from God, entrusted to us for a season and then surrendered to eternity.

We live in a culture that celebrates busyness. Full calendars are worn as badges of importance, and productivity often becomes a measure of worth. Over time, achievement becomes identity and performance becomes a way of proving our value. But the Sabbath interrupts that lie.

Every seventh day, God invites us to step away from our work and remember a truth that the world constantly tempts us to forget: Our value is not found in what we produce. For twenty-four hours, we cease from our ordinary labor and cares. We declare by our actions that the world continues without our striving.

The Sabbath reminds us that we are more than workers or producers. We are God’s beloved children. Because we belong to Him, we are free to rest. The rhythm of worship and rest teaches us that our worth is rooted not in performance but in God’s love.

#### ^[Rest as an Act of Trust]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

Many people think the Sabbath is primarily about stopping work. Biblically, it is something deeper. The Sabbath is about trusting God enough to stop.

When Israel journeyed through the wilderness, God provided manna from heaven each morning. The people were instructed to gather only enough for each day. If they attempted to store it, it spoiled. They were to trust that God would provide again tomorrow. On the sixth day, God provided a double portion and instructed His people to rest on the seventh day, trusting that He had already provided what they needed.

The lesson was never merely about food. It was about faith. Would they trust God’s provision enough to stop working and rest in His faithfulness? The same question confronts us today. Yet every Sabbath, God gently asks: “Do you trust Me enough to stop?”

Rest is not merely a physical experience; it is a spiritual posture—the quiet confidence that God is faithful, God is present, and God can be trusted. The psalmist captures this posture with God’s invitation: “Be still, and know that I am God” (Psalm 46:10, NKJV). As we practice Sabbath, anxious striving gives way to peaceful dependence, and the soul learns to rest in God.

#### ^[Remembering Who We Are]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

The fourth commandment begins with a single word: Remember. “Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy” (Exodus 20:8, NKJV). That command is significant because remembering has always been central to spiritual formation. We become what we repeatedly remember, and we drift from what we repeatedly forget.

Throughout Scripture, God repeatedly calls His people to remember who He is, what He has done, and who they are in relationship to Him. The Sabbath serves exactly that purpose. Every week it invites us to step out of the noise of life and remember realities that are easily forgotten.

The Sabbath calls us to remember that God is our Creator, Redeemer, and Sustainer. Life is His gift, salvation is His work, and our future is secure in His hands. The Sabbath teaches us to remember who God is, whose we are, and who we are becoming through His grace.

#### ^[Jesus Reveals the Heart of Sabbath]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

If the Sabbath reminds us who God is and whose we are, Jesus shows us what that relationship looks like in practice. Throughout His ministry, Jesus repeatedly taught, worshiped, healed, and served on the Sabbath. In doing so, He revealed not only how the Sabbath should be kept, but why it was given.

When questioned about His actions, Jesus declared, “The Sabbath was made for man, and not man for the Sabbath. Therefore the Son of Man is also Lord of the Sabbath” (Mark 2:27, 28, NKJV). These words remind us that the Sabbath is both Christ’s gift and Christ’s domain. It is ultimately about relationship with the Lord of the Sabbath Himself. It was designed for human flourishing and delight in God.

Jesus also revealed the Sabbath as a day of restoration. Time and again, He healed on the Sabbath, showing that God’s desire is wholeness, freedom, and life. The Sabbath points beyond itself to His ongoing work of restoration in human lives.

At its deepest level, the Sabbath is about relationship. Jesus consistently used the Sabbath to draw people closer to God. He revealed that the heart of true Sabbath keeping is entering into communion with the One who gives life. His invitation still stands today: “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28, NKJV). The deepest rest of the Sabbath is found not merely in ceasing from labor, but in coming to Christ Himself.

Sabbath invites us to set aside distractions and return our focus to God. He revealed that Sabbath is an ongoing opportunity to enter more deeply into the life of God. And as we enter that relationship, the Spirit continues His work of shaping us into the likeness of Christ.

#### ^[Preparing for Rest]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

The rhythm of worship and rest requires intentional preparation. Throughout Scripture, God’s people were encouraged to prepare for the sacred hours of Sabbath so they could enter them with greater freedom and delight.

Preparation is not about perfection but about creating space to receive God’s gift.

Three simple practices can help us enter more fully into the rhythm of worship and rest:

1. **Lay Down.** Before we can enter God’s rest, we must lay down both our work and our worries.
2. **Look Up.** The Sabbath creates space for worship. It lifts our eyes from the demands of life and redirects our attention to the One who gives life. Through worship, we remember who God is and what He has done.
3. **Lean In.** Sabbath strengthens our relationship with God and deepens our connection with others. We worship together, encourage one another, share meals, serve, and enjoy the gift of Christian fellowship.

These simple practices help us enter more fully into God’s gift of worship and rest.

#### ^[The Sabbath as a Means of Grace]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

God describes the Sabbath as more than a memorial of creation. It is also a sign of His sanctifying work. He declared, “Surely My Sabbaths you shall keep, . . . that you may know that I am the Lord who sanctifies you” (Exodus 31:13, NKJV). Centuries later, He repeated the same promise through Ezekiel: “Moreover I also gave them My Sabbaths, to be a sign between them and Me, that they might know that I am the Lord who sanctifies them” (Ezekiel 20:12, NKJV). The Sabbath reminds us that the God who created us is still at work within us.

Ellen G. White captures this beautifully when she writes that the Sabbath is a sign that “points to [God] as both the Creator and the Sanctifier.”<sup>1</sup>

God does not say the Sabbath is a sign of our superiority or merit. It is “a sign between them and Me”—a sign of relationship, belonging, and dependence. Each Sabbath we step away from our labor and quietly confess a truth that is easy to forget: We are deeply dependent upon God.

Every Sabbath is an act of humility. We stop. We surrender. We acknowledge: I am not self-sustaining. I am not self-saving. I am not self-sanctifying. We enter these holy hours anticipating His grace and participating in His transforming work. This is one of the great insights of sanctified stewardship.

Stewardship is our partnership with God in the work of our sanctification. The Sabbath becomes one of the primary places where that partnership is practiced. It is not merely about remembering the Creator; it is about cooperating with the Sanctifier.

Week after week, God uses this holy rhythm to remind us of His abundant grace and our continual need of His sanctifying presence. It calls us back to grace, trust, identity, and relationship with Him. The Sabbath is not merely a day we keep; it is a sacred space where the Beloved invites us into partnership with the Sanctifier. And through that sacred rhythm, God continues His work of forming Christ within us.

#### ^[A People Who Know How to Rest]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#436F83"}}})

The author of Hebrews speaks of a future Sabbath “rest for the people of God” (Hebrews 4:9, NKJV). Each weekly Sabbath is a glimpse of that coming rest—a foretaste of the wholeness, peace, and communion with God for which we were created.

We live in a restless world, yet every Sabbath whispers eternity into the present, calling us back to the assurance that we belong to God. The rhythm of worship and rest forms the soul as we learn to rest in God.

### ^[Appeal]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#5E0C1E"}}})

There comes a moment when truth must become a decision. And that moment is now.

The Creator who made you, the Redeemer who saved you, and the Sanctifier who is transforming you invites you into a rhythm of worship and rest.

Sabbath can feel difficult for busy souls. It feels unnatural in a culture that celebrates hurry, productivity, and constant activity. Yet if we are serious about sanctified stewardship, we must learn to rest as an act of faith and a form of worship.

Perhaps God is inviting you to lay something down—a burden, a schedule, a worry, or a striving spirit.

The invitation is not merely to stop working. It is to enter His rest. To trust His provision. To delight in His presence. To remember who He is, whose you are, and who you are becoming through His grace.

Will you accept His invitation?
