### ^[Lesson Study Intro]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#0070C0"}}})

This week’s lesson focuses on the origin of some of the key themes and symbols of Bible prophecy, specifically from the book of Genesis.

### ^[1. God’s Word is changeless and harmonious (Sab, Sun)]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#F19637"}}})

- God and His Word do not change (Is. 40:7, 8; Mal. 3:6; Heb. 13:8).
	- Accordingly, Revelation is not a stand-alone book but is the culmination of all Scripture.
		- “One of the key problems with modern interpretations of Bible prophecy, such as in Revelation, is that they fail to recognize the ancient roots of Revelation. The author assumes a knowledge of the Old Testament and uses concepts that would have been well-known to his audience.” Qtly, Sab, par 1
- ^[In the Revelation all the books of the Bible meet and end. AA 585]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#0070C0"}}})
- The themes and symbols of Bible prophecy retain the same meaning and relevance as when first invoked in Scripture.
	- This is sometimes called the law of principle of “first mention.”
		- “Generally speaking, the first time a concept or symbol is mentioned in the Bible—particularly in the opening chapters of Genesis—you will discover that it establishes a general understanding of that concept, which will help you understand how it’s being used later on.” Qtly, Sun, par 2

### ^[2. Prophetic themes/images about Satan are rooted in Genesis (Wed, Thu)]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#F19637"}}})

- Revelation’s dragon was Eden’s serpent (Rev. 12:7-9; Gen. 3:1-6).
- Through sin came death, the ultimate enemy of God’s people (Gen. 2:15-17; 1 Cor. 15:20-26).
	- The death of Abel prefigured the death of Christ, who now holds the keys to death (Rev. 1:18; see Qtly, Wed, par 5).

### ^[3. Prophetic themes/imagery about God are rooted in Genesis (Mon, Tue)]({"style": {"text": {"color":"#F19637"}}})

- Perhaps the most prominent and vital theme in the Bible and Bible prophecy is God’s character of love.
	- The first mention of love found in Scripture is in Genesis 22:2.
		- This has striking parallels to the first mention of love in the Gospel of John (Jn. 3:16).
		- “Abraham’s faith in God was such that he trusted Him, choosing to believe that God could raise his son if he went through with the sacrifice (Heb. 11:19). It foreshadowed God’s love for the human race. He loved us to the point where He ‘gave His only begotten Son’ (see Gen. 22:2, 12, 16)—and, then, raised Him from the dead. Thus, we are given a revelation of the kind of love, the self-sacrificing love, that God has for us.” Qtly, Mon, par 4
- The central figure in the book of Revelation is Jesus, the Lamb.
	- The first allusions to a substitutionary sacrifice that God would provide to redeem man were given in Eden (Gen. 3:15, 21).
	- Building on that Eden imagery, the first explicit mention of a sacrificial seh (the Hebrew word for lamb) also occurs in Genesis 22—the same story where the word love is first used.
		- “The first mention of a seh (lamb) in the Bible comes in the form of Isaac’s question: ‘Where is the lamb?’ (Gen. 22:7)… Meanwhile, look at the first mention of a Lamb in John’s Gospel, in John 1:29–34. It would almost seem as if John the Baptist is personally answering Isaac’s question.” Qtly, Tue, par 2, 3
	- All this symbolism culminates in Revelation’s nearly 30 references to the Lamb, emphasizing Christ as the central figure of Bible prophecy.
