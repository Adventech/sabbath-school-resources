> <p></p>
> With Jesus in His sanctuary

Earlier this year I met for breakfast with a young, gifted, and “on fire” Adventist pastor. He had requested a meeting, and I was happy to oblige. No sooner had I sat down than the questions came fast and furious: “What’s the deal with the sanctuary?” “Does it really matter?” “If it’s so important, and so biblically central, why is no one else emphasizing it the way we do?” “Is it just an artifact of our past that’s better left in the dustbin of history?”

An excellent, lively two-hour conversation ensued. Happily, there were many “aha” moments for the young minister (and me too!) in the course of our time together, but none greater than when I said this: “The sanctuary is all about Jesus—as lamb, priest, and king—and about God’s longing desire to dwell personally and permanently with His beloved people.”

The young man suddenly stopped, half-smiled, wrinkled his brow, shifted his eyes in obvious thought, and then, a few seconds later, blurted out, “Say that again!” So I did. And for more than a moment, he just sat there motionless, pensive, brow furrowed, taking it in.

“Wow,” he exclaimed. “I’ve never heard it like that. Can we talk through that?”

“Of course,” I responded.

What follows is a summary of what we talked about, so pull up a chair and enjoy the conversation.

### The Divine Desire to Dwell

God longs to be _with_ and _among_ His people. We see this beautiful truth about God’s heart unmistakably from Genesis to Revelation. In Genesis God fashions Adam and Eve in His own image, makes a garden for them to dwell in, and comes daily to visit them in their garden home. Furthermore, God creates the Sabbath, a day of rest, as a 24-hour “tabernacle in time.”

As Sigve K. Tonstad observes in _The Lost Meaning of the Seventh Day_: “The seventh day brings to view God’s priorities. Seeing that human time ‘begins with a day of rest and not a day of work,’ the spiritual pursuit, living life in a relationship with the Creator that is mutually meaningful, stands out as _the primary meaning in life_.”<sup>1</sup> Just one page later Tonstad writes, “The seventh day tells of _the importance of human beings to God_, and its primary message is not human duty but divine commitment.”<sup>2</sup> These insights are sublime, calibrating us to a proper understanding of the Sabbath as a pre-sin revelation of God’s heart, priorities, and faithfulness.

Unsurprisingly, the message of the Sabbath, a memorial of creation, is the same as the general message of creation: God loves and prioritizes His human children—those that specially bear His image—and accordingly He longs to be _with_ them. Fast-forwarding to Revelation, we see that the grand climax of Scripture and of God’s redemptive plan is that He is, at long last, with His beloved children from all ages.

Returning to the Old Testament, this time to Exodus, we again see God’s longing desire to be with and among His people, this time in the construction of the sanctuary: “And let them make Me a sanctuary, _that I may dwell among them_” (Ex. 25:8). Like creation and the Sabbath, the sanctuary brings to view God’s priorities. And what is among the highest of the divine priorities? To dwell with His people, to be among them.

Before we move on, just let the beauty and profundity of this simple and biblically central truth wash over you: God wants to be with you! Yes, you! He not only loves you, He likes you and wants you, with your unique and irreplaceable personality, to inhabit both eternity and your day-to-day life alongside Him. As our loving heavenly Father, God is for us, all of us. And He has made a way! There is no better news than this. It’s how Scripture opens and closes.

### A Pattern

The verse immediately after Exodus 25:8 says, “According to all that I show you, that is, the pattern of the tabernacle and the pattern of all its furnishings, just so you shall make it” (verse 9). The sanctuary design and structure (“the pattern”) was not Moses’ idea—it was God’s.<sup>3</sup>

The divinely given pattern was a three-part structure. The technical word for this, sometimes used by theologians, is tripartite, which simply means composed of, or involving, three parts. Those three parts of the sanctuary are the courtyard, the holy place, and the most holy place. The courtyard was just what it sounds like: a large fenced-in area, or yard. There was a single entrance to the courtyard, symbolizing that there is only one way—or one door—to access the sanctuary and, most important, the God who dwells there.

The courtyard contained two holy articles of furniture: a large altar and a basin of water called a laver. These, too, were designed by God, and again, the pattern was given to Moses. The holy place came next and contained three articles of furniture: a lampstand, a table with ceremonial bread, and a smaller altar for burning incense. The most holy place, which, curiously, was in the shape of a cube, contained a single article of furniture: the ark of the covenant, which held, among other things, the Ten Commandment tablets, written by God’s own hand and given to Moses.<sup>4</sup>

Eventually, about 1,000 years before the time of Christ, this temporary and mobile sanctuary became a permanent, immovable structure with the construction of King Solomon’s expansive and opulent temple. This temple, like the sanctuary before it, was modeled on the divinely given pattern and was gloriously and unmistakably inhabited by God Himself.

The sanctuary and its pattern communicated two grand realities: (1) God’s desire to be with His people and (2) His holiness requiring a certain way of dwelling with His people. Sin and uncleanness cannot come into the direct presence of God. As God told Moses: “You cannot see My face; for no [sinful] man shall see Me, and live” (Ex. 33:20). This being the case, God made _a way_ for Him to dwell with His sinful people, and that way was the sanctuary, with its specific and God-given pattern.

God knows that you are sinful and that you cannot rescue and restore yourself. So He made a way—a wonderful and wise way that doesn’t compromise His holiness or pretend that our sin isn’t real. It is a wondrous and beautiful solution that brings the sinner “up” to God through mercy, forgiveness, sacrifice, and restoration. God loves us dearly and is deadly serious (literally!) about saving us. Why? Because, again, He longs to be _with_ us personally and permanently!

### Jesus and the Sanctuary

The sanctuary, with all its beautiful and often mysterious symbolism, pointed to Jesus! It foreshadowed His incarnation, death, resurrection, and high-priestly ministry. Jesus is “the door” (John 10:7, 9) and “the way” (John 14:6), both references to Jesus as the one point of saving access to God, symbolized by the single entrance to the sanctuary.

Jesus is the “Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world,” as John the Baptizer boldly announced (John 1:29). Jesus is our “merciful and faithful High Priest” (Heb. 2:17) who “[sympathizes] with our weaknesses” and “was in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin” (Heb. 4:15). Jesus is the “bread of life” (John 6:35), a reference to both the manna in the wilderness and the ceremonial shewbread in the sanctuary. Jesus is “the light of the world” (John 8:12), a general reference to light and its illuminating properties, but also the sanctuary lampstand, which brought light to the interior of the sanctuary tent. Jesus is the “mercy seat,” the golden lid that covered the ark where the atoning blood was sprinkled by the high priest (also symbolizing Jesus) on the Day of Atonement (see Rom. 3:25, NET, and Lev. 16).<sup>5</sup>

In fact, Jesus is the whole sanctuary Himself! (See John 2:19-22; 1:14). One fascinating similarity between Jesus in His incarnation and the sanctuary is that both were rather plain and unimpressive viewed from the outside, but on the inside were filled with beauty, mystery, light, and holiness.<sup>6</sup>

One day Jesus told the astonished and utterly befuddled religious leaders of His time that “you search the Scriptures, for in them you think you have eternal life; and these are they which testify of Me. But you are not willing to come to Me that you may have life” (John 5:39, 40). On another occasion, following His resurrection, Jesus gave a Bible study to two of His bewildered and no-doubt thrilled disciples and said, “These are the words which I spoke to you while I was still with you, that all things must be fulfilled which were written in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms concerning Me” (Luke 24:44).

There can be little doubt that a large part of what Jesus meant on these two occasions (and others like them) is that the Old Testament sanctuary—its structure, various symbols, and overall message—pointed wonderfully and conclusively to Him: temple, door, lamb, high priest, bread, light, mercy seat, and more! Jesus is not just the one that so many Old Testament stories and symbols, including the sanctuary, _point to_. He is, Himself, _the point!_

Is Jesus “a mere doctrine”? Of course not! He is the second person of the Godhead, “the Son of God,” “the Savior of the World,” “the Word made flesh,” our “great High Priest,” our soon-returning “King of kings,” and more.<sup>7</sup> Jesus is the foundation and destination of the Christian faith, and the sanctuary points to Him and His various modes of ministry in multifarious and illuminating ways.
