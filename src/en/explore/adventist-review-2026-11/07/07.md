> When a church becomes a community interested in the lives of real people

No one expected the surprise that awaited at the White Memorial church’s Resurrection Sunday service—least of all Barry’s wife. For 15 years, out of love and commitment to his Adventist wife, he had attended church alongside her, but never out of his own conviction. A former Baha’i and a skeptic, Barry often scoffed at religion. It seemed, somehow, naive and unserious to him. But the day came that he saw his church in action, stepping into the brokenness of the city—serving, feeding, caring—and something about all of that opened his heart. Though for years he had been unmoved by the gospel they preached, he became captivated by the gospel they practiced.

### Barry: The Skeptic Who Longed for a Better World

To understand why this moment was so surprising, one must first understand Barry’s spiritual journey. As a Baha’i he was no stranger to conversations about faith, spirituality, religion, and even God. Yet a personal commitment to Christ was the furthest thing from his mind. His perception of Christianity was something of a caricature of faith—an institution more invested in power than in people, more complicit with injustice than resistant to it. Over time his skepticism deepened as he grew more sure that organized religion had little to offer a world marked by injustice and suffering.

When Barry married Arnie, he occasionally accompanied her to church. He sat, semi-politely, through sermons and classes, scoffing subtly from time to time. He served when needed, and connected, in a charming way, with members of the congregation. He always remained an attentive observer, but inwardly he just wasn’t buying it. It wasn’t people he was struggling to connect with; he struggled to connect with the concept of God and the framework of religion, which seemed silly to him, especially considering the pressing realities of people outside church walls.

Arnie never stopped praying. Neither did her parents, nor other members of the congregation who came to know and love Barry. They were patient, trusting that the Holy Spirit was at work in ways they could not yet see. And through their own lives they provided a compelling picture of what it means to live as disciples of Jesus. As they prayed on, Barry remained present and active in the life of the church, but he had not encountered a gospel that felt tangible and compelling enough to embrace as his own.

### A Church That Showed Up

The turning point in Barry’s story began, not with a sermon, but with a church willing to rethink the way it did mission. Fifteen years of prayer were about to be answered in an unexpected way. White Memorial pivoted to a ministry model that aligned with Ellen White’s well-known description of Christ’s method of ministry. She says that He “mingled with [people] as one who desired their good. He showed His sympathy for them, ministered to their needs, and won their confidence.”* As she explains it, this all came before He extended the invitation to follow Him.

This shift proved to be more than a ministry strategy; it reoriented the church’s entire identity. Rather than waiting for people to come to the church, the church increasingly entered the life of the city through neighborhood partnerships, community events, and meaningful presence in the life and rhythms of the city. Barry noticed. He watched church members build genuine relationships with people beyond their own circles, making a space for people from all walks of life. It felt genuine to him. He started seeing the church as less of an institution he attended, and more of a community interested in the lives of real people.

### A Church That Listened

As the church learned to mingle more intentionally with its neighbors, it also learned to listen—a discipline that earns a congregation credibility in its community. Following Christ’s example meant paying attention to the narratives, fears, struggles, joys, and hopes of the people around it. Alongside its evangelistic vision, the church embraced a deeper commitment to seeking the shalom (flourishing and wholeness) of the community, as God instructed the people of Israel, in Jeremiah 29:7, when they were exiled in Babylon. Members found that this “seeking the shalom” felt more aligned with the method of Christ, who chose to meet practical needs and build relationships before extending invitations to follow Him.

Barry noticed as the church practiced sympathy—sitting with people’s pain and valuing their humanity without expecting anything in return. This began to challenge one of his deepest reservations about organized religion, namely, that it often talked more than it listened. Suddenly compassion wasn’t just a talking point in a sermon, but an embodied reality that was changing people’s lives—including his own.

### A Church That Served

Presence and listening ought to lead to action. There is no church without ministry. White Memorial committed to embodying Christ’s love by tangibly meeting the needs of the city. Its members grew increasingly engaged in supporting the underserved, accompanying immigrant families, advocating for the dignity of the marginalized, and standing alongside those navigating uncertainty and hardship. Through food distributions, support of the vulnerable, and ministries of justice and compassion, the church demonstrated that the gospel is more than simply a ticket to a blissful afterlife; it addresses the whole person. When devastating fires swept through Los Angeles in 2025, members mobilized quickly, organized relief efforts, and helped approximately 5,000 families.

Barry watched his church step into the pain of the city, and suddenly religion felt more credible to him. Maybe Christianity wasn’t as self-serving as he had thought. He was seeing a gospel far more powerful than what he had known in the past, and Jesus was becoming impossible to dismiss.

### A Church That Earned Trust

Trust takes time. For Barry it wasn’t won through a persuasive Bible study or a compelling sermon, but through years of faithful presence. He was loved by a community that was committed to reflecting Christ’s love through prayer, compassion, and genuine friendship. And the church’s love for its city demonstrated to him that its members truly cared for people, not simply for increasing their ranks.

Slowly Barry’s assumptions about Christianity began to change. The faith he once saw as disconnected from the real world he now began to recognize as being deeply invested in its healing. Confidence was eventually won. It wasn’t in a single conversation, but through a long period of faithful presence and visible love. Somehow, now, for Barry, the idea of following Christ no longer seemed unreasonable. There was something likable about this Jesus, and Barry found himself drawn to Him.

### A Man Who Followed

Barry, the quiet observer, eventually became convicted. No one pressured him. No one insisted on baptism. The church simply loved. Barry saw a gospel being lived out in ways he could no longer ignore, and he found it irresistible. The Christ he encountered while loving the city was the same Christ he now wanted to follow with his whole life.

Quietly he had held these thoughts as conviction grew. Then during one Resurrection Sunday service came the moment no one saw coming—except the pastor who had conspired with Barry to surprise the entire church, including Arnie. That morning the members gathered to celebrate the risen Savior. After 11 beautiful baptisms, the pastor revealed that there would be one more—an Easter surprise. Barry came forward. The church gasped, and Arnie nearly collapsed. What felt like an ordinary Resurrection Sunday celebration became the answer to 15 years of prayer. This was more than a baptism; this was a victory. It was a joyful culmination of years of patient witness, embodied compassion, and the quiet, gentle work of the Holy Spirit.

### A City Church and Its Mission

Barry’s story reminds us that urban mission is possible. It begins by entering the life of the city, listening, meeting needs, earning trust, and eventually inviting people to follow Jesus. Christ’s method remains as relevant in today’s cities as when He first practiced it in the dusty streets of Capernaum and Jerusalem. In our cities, rich with diversity, yet marked by skepticism, injustice, and loneliness, the church’s credibility is strengthened when the gospel is embodied before it’s proclaimed.

As we seek the well-being of our communities through justice, compassion, and faithful presence, we create a space for the Spirit to accomplish what only He can. Barry’s baptism is a reminder that transformed cities and transformed lives often begin the same way: with people willing to live out the gospel before asking others to believe it.
