> Embracing God’s radical call

It was December 1844 in Portland, Maine. A group of five women knelt in prayer, their hearts bruised by grief. Just two months prior, on October 22, their world had shattered. Thousands of Millerite Advent-ists had watched the sun set and rise again without the appearance of their Savior. The Great Disappointment had left them ridiculed, bewildered, and spiritually cast awry.

In that room knelt a 17-year-old girl named Ellen Harmon. Weighing barely 80 pounds, chronically ill from a childhood head injury that had ended her formal education at age 9, and possessing a voice so damaged she could barely speak above a whisper, she was the epitome of human frailty. Yet, as she prayed, the room seemed to dissolve. She felt a power pass through her, lifting her above the dark world, where she saw a straight and narrow path casting a bright light behind the Advent people. Jesus was leading them toward the Holy City.<sup>1</sup>

This was the birth of a prophetic ministry that would span seven decades, shape a global movement, and provide a living template for how God communicates with a fallen world. But more than an isolated historical event, Ellen Harmon’s (later White’s) first vision illuminates an eternal truth that runs from Genesis to Revelation: When God calls an individual to His service, He is not looking for human résumés, cultural prestige, or societal validation. The Holy Spirit anoints whom He will, and whomever He calls is His absolute ideal—not a second choice or a desperate last resort.

### Forging Messengers From Flawed Clay

To understand the radical nature of divine calling, we must look backward into the biblical narrative. God has never chosen His representatives based on human standards of readiness. Rather, the history of salvation is a gallery of unlikely candidates, each called at a precise geopolitical and spiritual inflection point to realign a wandering people with the everlasting covenant and renew their characters to be as God’s character.

#### Abraham: The Flawed Guardian of the Promise

Consider Abram, later renamed Abraham. God did not find him in a sanctuary of pristine theological purity; He called him out of Ur of the Chaldees, a metropolis steeped in polytheism and moon worship. When God called him to leave his country and his kindred for an unnamed inheritance, Abraham went, stepping into the dark on nothing but a divine promise (see Heb. 11:8).

Adventist theology has long recognized that Abraham’s justification was rooted entirely in faith, akin to the righteousness by faith that anchors the three angels’ messages (Gen. 15:6; Gal. 3:6-9). Yet Abraham was a deeply flawed man. He lied about his wife to foreign kings out of sheer self-preservation, and he tried to engineer God’s promises through Hagar.

But when the ultimate, excruciating test came on Mount Moriah—the command to offer Isaac—Abraham’s full submission was laid bare. As Seventh-day Adventist pioneer writer J. N. Andrews once noted, the sacrifice on Moriah was not merely a test of blind obedience, but a visceral revelation of the plan of salvation, showing the father of the faithful the agonizing depth of the sacrifice the heavenly Father would make in giving His own Son.<sup>2</sup> Abraham was not God’s second-best option for founding a covenant nation; despite his flaws, his capacity for radical surrender made him the ideal instrument.

#### Elijah: The Voice in the Wilderness of Apostasy

Centuries later, when Israel had succumbed to the cultural and spiritual rot of Baal worship under Ahab and Jezebel, God did not dispatch a polished diplomat or a high priest from Jerusalem. He called Elijah—a rugged, uncourtly Tishbite from the hills of Gilead. Elijah’s call was defined by confrontation. He stood on Mount Carmel, a solitary figure against 450 prophets of Baal and 400 prophets of Asherah, making a Spirit-filled appeal for national repentance (1 Kings 18:19-39).

The Elijah message was simple yet devastating: How long will you falter between two opinions? Elijah was the ideal messenger because his complete detachment from courtly politics made him unbought and unbossed. He was a human lightning rod for the Holy Spirit, turning the hearts of the people back to the true God before the fire fell.

#### Isaiah: The Cleansed Lips of Majesty

When Isaiah beheld the terrifying majesty of God in the heavenly temple, his immediate reaction was not professional ambition, but a profound awareness of his own ruin. “Woe is me,” he cried, “for I am undone! Because I am a man of unclean lips” (Isa. 6:5).

Isaiah could not fully serve until he experienced the agonizing, purifying touch of the live coal from the altar upon his mouth. Only after this radical cleansing, when the triune God asked, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for Us?” did Isaiah respond with those iconic words of absolute availability: “Here am I! Send me” (verse 8). Isaiah’s qualification did not rest in his natural eloquence, but in his willingness to be shattered and remade by the presence of Yahweh.

#### John The Baptist: The Ascetic Bridge

At the threshold of the New Testament stands John the Baptist, a man whose clothing of camel’s hair and diet of locusts and wild honey intentionally mirrored Elijah. John’s calling required him to step outside every comfort zone of first-century Judean society. He rejected the traditional priestly career paths of his lineage to live in the desert, preaching a harsh but beautiful message of repentance to prepare the way for the Messiah (Mark 1:2-5).

Even when imprisoned by Herod—battling the dark shadows of doubt that Satan uses against every tired messenger—John remained Christ’s ideal forerunner. Jesus Himself affirmed that among those born of women, there had not risen anyone greater than John (Matt. 11:11).

### An Unlikely Messenger for a Shattered Movement

When we transition from the biblical canon to the birth of the Seventh-day Adventist Church, the divine methodology remains stunningly consistent. In the wake of October 22, 1844, the shattered Millerite movement needed a voice to guide them through the theological fog. There were prominent men available—intellectual giants, eloquent preachers, and such capable administrators as Joseph Bates or James White.

Instead, the Holy Spirit bypassed the strong, the educated, and the socially influential. God chose Ellen Harmon.

Think of the staggering improbability of this choice. In 1844, American society was deeply patriarchal; women were denied the right to vote, legal autonomy, and the right to speak in most public religious assemblies. Furthermore, Ellen was only 17 years old—a teenager whose physical health was so compromised that her lungs were hemorrhaging, and physicians believed she had only weeks to live. She was poor and possessed a third-grade education.

By every human metric of leadership, she was a noncandidate. When the call came, she shrank from it in terror, praying that God would lay the burden on someone else. When Ellen finally surrendered her weakness to God’s strength, her liability became the exact medium through which God’s power could be magnified without human pride obscuring the view. Because she was weak and possessed a limited education, no one could credit her profound theological insights, her sweeping historical visions, or her timeless counsels on health and education to her own genius. The beauty of her ministry was a living fulfillment of Pauline theology: God chooses the foolish things of the world to put to shame the wise (1 Cor. 1:27). Whomever He calls is His ideal, designed precisely for the unique demands of the hour.

### The Spirit’s Anointing: A Call to Every Believer

This brings us to the urgent, contemporary reality that addresses every person sitting in our pews today. The prophetic voice of the past is not merely a historical artifact to be admired from a distance; it is a mirror reflecting our own divine responsibilities. In the last moments of earth’s history, God’s people must understand that the call of God is not an elite privilege reserved for the few, but a universal mandate extended to all.

We must let the Holy Spirit be the agency who calls and equips. Far too often our local churches fall into the trap of corporatization, attempting to box the Spirit’s anointing into categories of human prejudice, educational credentials, age brackets, or cultural backgrounds. But the economy of heaven operates on a completely different blueprint: “There are diversities of gifts, but the same Spirit. There are differences of ministries, but the same Lord. And there are diversities of activities, but it is the same God who works all in all. But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to each one for the profit of all” (1 Cor. 12:4-7).

The apostle Paul’s words to the Corinthian church are an absolute declaration of spiritual inclusivity. The Holy Spirit is radically egalitarian in His distribution of gifts. He is gender-inclusive, age-inclusive, and race-inclusive.

When the Spirit seeks an instrument to launch a global health ministry, a local community outreach, an innovative digital evangelism initiative, or a ministry of quiet, intercessory prayer, He does not consult our cultural hierarchies. He does not check whether an individual is male or female, a Ph.D. academic or an undereducated laborer, a septuagenarian or a 17-year-old youth.

If you are sitting in a pew today feeling that your age renders you irrelevant, remember Ellen White at 17. If you feel that your lack of wealth or formal education disqualifies you, remember the fishermen of Galilee and the preacher in the desert. God has never been a respecter of persons (Acts 10:34). If He has placed a burden upon your heart for a specific task to advance His kingdom, you are not His fallback option because someone more qualified said no. You are His ideal choice for that specific sphere of influence.

### Stepping Outside the Comfort Zone

Accepting this call, however, requires us to abandon the pursuit of a comfortable, sanitized Christianity. A call from God is rarely an invitation to a path of least resistance. Like the ancient prophets, our personal assignments will almost certainly push us far outside our comfort zones. It may require substantial material sacrifice; it will certainly demand deep humility and a daily, agonizing full submission of our personal wills to the sovereign will of God.

You may not be called to the canonical or noncanonical prophetic office of a Daniel or an Ellen White. But you are called to be a living prophetic voice within your home, your workplace, your university, and your neighborhood. You are called to embody the Elijah message—to visually demonstrate the beauty of Christ’s righteousness in a world drowning in secular materialism and moral confusion.

This work cannot be accomplished by human grit, intellectual acrobatics, or pastoral charisma. The task before us is humanly impossible. The cultural currents of our post-Christian era are too strong, the opposition too sophisticated, and our own resources too meager.

But herein lies our enduring hope: He who calls you is faithful, who also will do it (1 Thess. 5:24). God qualifies the called. When we surrender our meager “five loaves and two fish” of talent, time, and identity to the Master, the Holy Spirit anoints that offering with infinite power.

Let us stop hiding behind our perceived inadequacies. Let us silence the voice of the enemy that whispers we are too young, too old, too broken, or too uneducated to matter. The King of kings is walking down the aisles of our lives today, looking past our outward appearances directly into our hearts. He is asking once again, “Whom shall I send?”

May we have the courage, the humility, and the faith to look up through our weakness and reply, “Here am I! Send me.”
